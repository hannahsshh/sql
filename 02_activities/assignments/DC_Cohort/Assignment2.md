The article makes a number of interesting points around the reality of human work inherently integrated into the foundation of neural nets and their continued involvement in their function. 
The author provides a number of example of human labour that have been foundational to the development of neural nets that have largely gone unnoticed. The ability to develop necessary training materials for AI algorithms depends on the creation of a massive dataset of somethings things that can be appropriately classified as either “right” or “wrong”. The classifiers, people, provide manual labour to generate these binary answers so that an algorithm can subsequently be tested against it. Depending on the specific use case, millions of images/datasets/etc. may be required to train an algorithm correctly. The individual human labour used to make ImageNet was not recognized, each person who devoted their time is not recognized as an active participant in the process of generating the dataset. As datasets grow and function on a quasi-crowdsourcing basis, the human labour underlying this fundamental first step toward the creation of reliable algorithms cannot and will not be specifically recognized. Is the true architect of an algorithm then the person overseeing the project or the thousands (and more) individuals providing the essential resource for its creation? 
This question leads into the more pressing concern discussed in the article, around the reliability of this generated data. There is a quote included in the assigned reading which brings this essential question forward. As Dr. Li is rethinking ImageNet, the following quote is included to describe the advancement: “we have been conducting a research project to systematically identify and remedy fairness issues that resulted from the data collection process in the person subtree of ImageNet.” But how can fairness be systematically remedied? The concept of fairness is famously and rather unfortunately subjective; there is no universal definition of fair. Societal and systemic discrimination based on any characteristic (e.g. race, sex, religion, ability, appearance) can be conceptualized as a discrepancy in what some consider “fair”. A person who holds negative core beliefs about an “other” (whether person, group of people, etc.) would not have the same conception of fairness as it relates to their description of that “other”. For example, person who has fundamentally misogynistic beliefs would certainly consider it fair to describe a woman as weak or less intelligent. 
So on whose definition of fairness is the ImageNet platform being fixed? Who are the moderators and on what value system are moderations made? As mentioned before, a binary “right” and “wrong” dataset is needed to train an algorithm. This is easier (though not necessarily foolproof) for something basic and visual, like a hotdog, as per the article’s example. Almost anything beyond that, and perhaps most importantly the presentation of a person, is full of nuance and context, things we are consistently challenged by as human beings. If all the way down, neural nets are just people, the foundation of any neural net will be rife with judgements made by those people. The mission of systemically applying fairness within algorithms remains dependent on who defines the “fairness” applied in the task, and whether or not we personally may agree with the principle, that value is not as objective as we want to believe.
