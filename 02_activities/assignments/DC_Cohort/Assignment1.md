The article assigned is a very clear example of how social norms and societal values are embedded into database architecture. The entire system of the CNIC is built on the foundation that a single, normative family structure is the “default” (i.e. correct) setup of the society and therefore should be universal, lest people exist in “wrong” family structures. The idea is ultimately punitive in that anyone existing beyond the “correct” structure is unworthy and unable to access rights, services, etc. As humans design and maintain databases and collect data itself, these fundamental social principles are integrated because the systems reflect the inherent biases of their creators. 
In my own work as a health researcher, one of my primary areas of focus is the ongoing challenge of sex as a biological variable in research. This challenge is reflected in both how data is collected from the earliest preclinical study to large multi-centre clinical trials, to the research priorities set out by governments that impact funding and policy. The longstanding patriarchal values and systems existing around health and healthcare continue to arise as algorithmic and AI tools become popularized. It is a basic truth that data on men’s health, disease symptoms, responses to treatment, etc. have been prioritized and collected at a substantially higher volume than data related to women, and greater disparities exist in the collection of data relating to conditions that primarily impact those assigned female at birth. Well publicized and known symptoms of common conditions such as strokes, for example, reflect those experienced by men. These same data that have led to the colloquially recognizable symptoms of stroke will be applied to new healthcare tools designed to “predict” those most at risk of stroke. The same can be said for a variety of common conditions, as they have been studied in preclinical studies using male non-human animals, examined using male human data in clinical trials, and treatments have been tested almost exclusively in males. Thus, the body of evidence that can inform new technologies is male-centric to the detriment of any specific female biological mechanisms, clinical symptoms, and treatment responses. In a patriarchal society, men are the default condition. Algorithms trained on the “best available data” are therefore being trained on the male information collected and maintained in our human-designed databases. 
Similar examples exist for other marginalized groups. Dermatology, for example, is a field plagued by inequities that are exacerbated by data systems that take “Caucasian” as the default value. For years, diagnostic criteria for benign and pathological skin conditions have been built on databases that contain systemic racial biases. For example, the classification of risk for moles is designed based on decades of data collected on patients that are primarily Caucasian. This has led to training materials for physicians only containing visuals of these conditions on white skin and a profound disparity in how non-white patients are able to get early diagnoses and appropriate healthcare. Clinical databases are reflective of societal biases in that data must be collected to be included, and racialized groups continue to face discrimination and accessibility issues in healthcare systems, that among many things, prevents their inclusion in clinical studies. 
Whether intentional or not, “default” parameters of society are reflective of some of the worst elements of discrimination and are regularly and automatically integrated into the systems we design. Establishing these “defaults” within data systems, whether related to healthcare or personal identification, will ultimately lead to exclusion. Any subsequent tool or service built on such foundation will continue to fail those who are excluded from the “default” and further exacerbate the existing disparities.
